How to Buy Used Motorcycle Gear Safely

Buying used motorcycle gear is one of the smartest ways to get quality protection at a fraction of retail cost. Quality leather jackets, boots, and race suits hold up for years — the materials don't degrade on a shelf. But there are things to check and things to avoid. This guide covers everything you need to know.

The #1 Rule: Never Buy Crashed Gear

Motorcycle gear — especially jackets, pants, and suits — is designed to absorb impact energy. Once it takes a hit, the protective materials (foam, armor, leather) are compromised, even if they look intact. On UsedMotorcycleGear.com, every seller must certify that their gear has never been in a crash before listing. Never buy gear from someone who can't confirm this.

Inspecting a Used Motorcycle Jacket

Check the armor

Remove the CE armor from the shoulder, elbow, and back pockets. It should be firm with no cracks, crumbles, or deformation. Soft D3O-style armor should feel pliable and springy. Hard-shell armor should show no fractures. If the armor feels brittle or crumbles at the edges, replace it before wearing.

Inspect the outer shell

For leather: scuffs and scratches are cosmetic, but look for any patches or repaired areas — these are red flags for a previous crash. Abrasion wear on the elbows or shoulders (the first contact points in a slide) is a serious warning sign. For textile: check for tears, delamination of waterproof membranes, and worn-through outer fabric on high-friction areas.

Check the seams and zippers

Seams should be double- or triple-stitched with no fraying. Pull gently at the shoulder and elbow seams — they should not separate. All zippers (main zip, vents, pockets, the pants-to-jacket zipper) should run smoothly. A stuck zipper is an easy DIY fix with zipper lubricant; a broken zipper pull or slider needs replacement.

Fit for riding, not standing

A motorcycle jacket should fit in the riding position: arms forward, slightly crouched. Stand with your arms out as if gripping handlebars — the jacket should feel comfortable with armor sitting over the correct joints. If the shoulders or elbows pull when reaching forward, the jacket is too small. If armor migrates off-joint, it's too large.

Inspecting Used Motorcycle Gloves

Palm and knuckle protection

Check the palm slider (scaphoid protector) if present — it should move freely and show no cracks. Knuckle armor should be firm with no splits. Foam padding inside the palm should be intact, not compressed flat.

Stitching and wear

Examine fingertip stitching — this wears fastest from grip friction. Check the wrist closure (velcro or strap) for full function. The wrist should close snugly; gloves that are loose at the wrist will come off in a crash.

Inspecting Used Motorcycle Boots

Ankle protection

Press on the internal ankle protection (usually rigid or semi-rigid cups). They should feel solid, not crumbled or absent. CE-rated ankle protection is a key feature — boots without it offer significantly less protection in a crash.

Sole and upper

The sole should be firmly bonded — press the toe and heel to check for separation. Worn-through soles affect grip and can be resoled by a cobbler if the upper is in good shape. Check the toe and heel shift pad (the area that contacts the shifter and heel) for wear depth.

Closure systems

Test all buckles, zippers, velcro, and lace systems. Boots need to fit snugly around the ankle — a loose boot will rotate in a crash. Lace-up closures are the most adjustable; zipper closures are the most convenient.

Inspecting Used Motorcycle Pants

Knee and hip armor

Remove and inspect knee and hip armor — same checks as jacket armor. Knee armor must sit over your actual knee when wearing the pants in a riding position; adjust the armor pocket height if needed (most quality pants allow this).

Inner thigh and seat wear

The inner thigh and seat areas take the most abrasion wear in normal use from saddle friction. Check for thinning fabric or worn-through patches in these areas on textile pants. On leather, look for glazing or significant thinning. Cosmetic wear is fine; structural thinning is not.

Inspecting a Used Motorcycle Suit

Apply all the jacket and pants checks above. Additionally, test the main zipper from end to end — it's the most critical structural element and the hardest to repair. For two-piece suits, test the connecting zipper that joins the jacket to the pants; it must hold reliably to prevent the jacket from riding up in a crash. Inspect the hump (if present) — a damaged back hump can indicate a crash.

What to Avoid Buying

  • Gear the seller can't confirm is crash-free
  • Any gear with patches, repairs, or repaired stitching over abrasion areas
  • Helmets (single-impact protection only — never buy used)
  • Armor that crumbles, cracks, or feels brittle when flexed
  • Boots with separated soles or compromised ankle cups
  • Gear listed without photos of the interior armor pockets
  • Anything priced suspiciously low from an unverified seller
